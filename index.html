What a proof looks like when a computer checks it

You already know what a proof is. A formal proof is one written so precisely that a program can check every step, down to the axioms. This page explains how that works in Lean, using a handful of short proofs. You can step through each one, and every line of Lean shown was checked by Lean itself.

01

What is proof formalization?

Formalization means writing definitions, statements and proofs in a language with fixed rules, so that checking a proof becomes a mechanical task a computer can do.

A proof on paper is written for a reader. It skips routine steps (“clearly”, “by symmetry”, “similarly”) and relies on shared conventions. That is a feature, not a flaw, but it means a paper proof is checked only as carefully as its readers check it.

A formal proof leaves nothing implicit. Every object has a precise definition, every statement has an exact form, and every step is justified by a rule of logic, an axiom, or an earlier theorem. A small program called a proof checker then confirms that each step follows. If the checker accepts, the theorem follows from the axioms, with no gaps.

Writing every step by hand would be unbearable. Proof assistants such as Lean therefore let you write high-level steps (“rewrite with this equation”, “induct on n”, “this is linear arithmetic”) and fill in the low-level details automatically. The result is still checked to the last detail.

Why bother? For certainty about long or delicate arguments; for a library of mathematics that can be searched and reused; for collaboration in which nobody has to re-check anybody else's lemmas; and because it shows you exactly what your proof uses.

Some landmarks

2005 · Coq

Four Colour Theorem

Gonthier and Werner formalized the 1976 computer-assisted proof, including the case analysis that no human had checked by hand.

2014 · HOL Light, Isabelle

Kepler conjecture

The Flyspeck project, led by Hales, completed a formal proof after referees of the original proof could not certify it in full.

2022 · Lean

Liquid Tensor Experiment

Scholze challenged the community to check a key theorem of condensed mathematics. It was done in Lean, and the formalization helped clarify the proof.

2024– · Lean

Fermat's Last Theorem

A large ongoing project led by Kevin Buzzard to formalize a modern proof of FLT, building on Mathlib.

02

What is Lean?

Lean is a programming language and an interactive theorem prover. You state a theorem and write a proof, and Lean either accepts it or tells you exactly what is still missing.

Leonardo de Moura started Lean at Microsoft Research in 2013. The current version, Lean 4, is developed by the Lean FRO, a non-profit founded in 2023. Its logic is a dependent type theory (a variant of the calculus of inductive constructions). In practice this means three things: mathematical objects have types, propositions are types too, and a proof of a proposition is an object of that type.

“Interactive” is the important word. While you write a proof, Lean shows the goal: what you know (the hypotheses) and what you still have to show, written after the turnstile ⊢. Each line of a tactic proof changes the goal, until nothing is left. The examples below let you watch this happen, one line at a time.

How Lean checks a proof

You writeStatement + proof

Usually as tactics: intro, rw, apply, induction…

→
ElaboratorBuilds a proof term

Runs the tactics, infers implicit arguments, finds structures such as “this is a group”. Large and clever, but not trusted.

→
Kernel · trustedChecks the term

A small program that checks one thing: that the term really has the type of the statement.

→
ResultTheorem added

Now it can be cited by later proofs, just like a lemma in a paper.

This split is the reason to trust the result. Tactics and automation can be arbitrarily complicated, and can be wrong, but whatever they produce is re-checked by the kernel. A bug in a tactic can stop it from finding a proof. It cannot make Lean accept a false theorem.

03

What is Mathlib?

Mathlib is Lean's community library of formalized mathematics: algebra, analysis, topology, number theory, combinatorics, category theory and more, all checked by Lean and all usable in your own proofs.

It began in 2017 for Lean 3 and was ported to Lean 4 in 2023. Hundreds of mathematicians and computer scientists contribute, and every change is reviewed and must compile together with everything else. This is why a formal proof of Euclid's theorem can be eleven lines: facts such as “m ≤ n implies m ∣ n!” are already there.

Names that say what they state

Mathlib lemmas are named after their statements: mul_one is a·1 = a, inv_mul_cancel is a⁻¹a = 1. Once you know the convention you can often guess a name, and tools such as exact? search the library for you. These are the Mathlib facts used on this page, printed by Lean's #check:

Note how general they are. mul_one is stated for any MulOneClass, so it applies to groups, rings, ℕ, matrices and anything else that has a multiplication with a 1. That is why the group proofs below can use it unchanged.

An error

n + 1 = n is false, so no proof exists. omega searches, fails, and reports a counterexample to the constraints it was given. The file does not compile.

Lean then carries on checking the rest of the file. To do that it fills the failed proof with a placeholder, which is why false_claim is also listed as depending on sorryAx. A failed proof can never pass for a real one.

sorry: an IOU

sorry closes any goal without proof. It is useful while you work, but Lean warns about it, and the theorem is then recorded as depending on the axiom sorryAx. The next section shows how that dependence is detected.

07

What a certificate means

“Lean accepted it” is a precise claim. A certificate records exactly what was checked, by what, and with which axioms, so that anyone can repeat the check.

For this page the certificate has three parts:

  • What: the exact source files, identified by their SHA-256 hashes. Change one character and the hash changes.
  • By what: the exact Lean version and the exact Mathlib commit.
  • Assuming what: for each theorem, the axioms its proof ultimately depends on, as reported by Lean's #print axioms. This walks through every lemma the proof uses, all the way down through Mathlib.

The axioms you will see

  • propext: logically equivalent propositions are equal. Tactics such as rw often produce proofs that use it.
  • Quot.sound: equivalent elements of a quotient are equal. Function extensionality follows from it.
  • Classical.choice: the axiom of choice. It also gives the law of the excluded middle, and so proof by contradiction.
  • no axioms The proof is purely constructive. It uses only the rules of the type theory itself.
  • sorryAx Not a proof. Somewhere there is a gap, or an error that Lean recovered from.

The first three are Lean's standard axioms, and almost all of Mathlib uses them. They correspond to ordinary mathematics with the axiom of choice.

What it does not certify

Lean checks that the proof proves the statement as written. Whether that statement means what you intend is a human judgement. Suppose, in the identity theorem, we had written only e * a = a (a left identity) for e and only a * e' = a (a right identity) for e′. That theorem is still true, but it is a different theorem. Reading the statement carefully is the one step a checker cannot do for you.

You also trust the kernel and the machine that ran it. The kernel is small and well studied, and independent re-checkers such as lean4checker can replay the proofs through it again.
